# EQ, filters, frequency and Q

## EQ asks which frequencies need a different balance
Equalization changes the relative level of frequency regions. It can make an arrangement clearer or solve a tonal problem, but it does not automatically make a performance more consistent in loudness over time. That latter problem often calls for level editing or compression. A filter is a particular frequency-shaping tool: a high-pass attenuates lower frequencies, a low-pass attenuates higher frequencies, and a shelf changes a broad side of the spectrum.

A parametric bell is commonly described by center frequency, gain and Q. Frequency selects the neighborhood, gain determines boost or cut, and Q controls how concentrated the change is. Higher Q generally affects a narrower neighborhood; lower Q spreads the change more broadly. A high-pass is not synonymous with a high-frequency boost. Raising the cutoff may remove wanted body as well as unwanted rumble.

## Diagnose before choosing a curve
Listen in the arrangement, name the problem and make one reversible change. For example, ask whether the vocal is masked, whether a resonance rings, or whether the entire capture is dull. A broad imbalance and a narrow ringing note need different experiments. Bypass at comparable loudness after each change; a boost can seem better merely because it adds level.

1. Save the unprocessed recording or keep a bypassable insert.
2. Find whether the problem is broad, narrow or caused by another instrument.
3. Choose an appropriate filter shape and a modest change.
4. Check consonants, warmth and the surrounding tracks.
5. Reduce the change if it fixes one moment but harms the rest.

## Original worked example
Imagine two spoken recordings: one has steady low-frequency desk vibration, while the other only becomes harsh on occasional loud words. A cautiously placed high-pass might address the first problem if useful voice content remains. A permanent treble cut can dull every word in the second recording without specifically managing the loud events. Compare targeted level or dynamic processing instead of assuming that both are simply EQ problems.

## Common misconceptions
A narrow notch is not automatically more natural, and every track does not need the same low cut. A visually flat analyzer is not a required musical goal. This guide provides no universal vocal frequency chart. Exact cutoff, bandwidth and gain choices depend on the source, arrangement and processing; the listening method is more transferable than a copied preset.
