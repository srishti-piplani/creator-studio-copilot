# Recording, editing, comping and crossfades

## Preserve choices before making edits
Recording captures a performance, while editing changes how captured material is arranged or presented. Useful preparation includes an intelligible monitoring path, sufficient input headroom and a short playback check. Keep original takes so that an edit can be revised without asking the performer to reproduce an unavailable moment. A polished edit cannot supply a syllable that was never recorded.

Comping builds a composite performance from selected portions of multiple takes. Evaluate phrasing, timing, tone and continuity rather than choosing the loudest fragment at every boundary. It is possible to assemble individually attractive words that no longer form a believable sentence. Listen through the transitions without stopping after every selection.

## Boundary treatment and crossfades
At a join, a sudden waveform discontinuity can produce a click. A fade changes level near an edge; a crossfade overlaps a fade-out and a fade-in. This can smooth a transition, but it is not a guarantee of coherent timing or tone. A very long crossfade across different spoken words can expose two performances at once. Closely related signals can also interact during an overlap, so the curve and placement should be auditioned rather than chosen solely by appearance.

1. Duplicate or version the edit before replacing source regions.
2. Select complete phrases where possible, keeping breath and consonant context.
3. Align intended timing before refining the joins.
4. Add short, appropriate transitions and listen for clicks or doubled syllables.
5. Play the whole passage in the mix, then inspect the final export.

## Original scenario
A good first sentence comes from take one and a confident second sentence from take three. Their room tone and breath timing differ. Move the transition to a natural pause, preserve suitable ambience, and compare a brief crossfade with a hard join. If the transition still sounds artificial, choose another take boundary instead of lengthening the crossfade indefinitely.

## Common misconceptions
Comping is not automatically pitch correction, and a crossfade cannot erase every recording problem. Non-destructive editing means the source may remain available; it does not eliminate the need for backups or careful file management. This guide deliberately avoids exact menu paths, fixed fade lengths and a universal comping algorithm. The goal is a reversible decision process that preserves performance continuity.
