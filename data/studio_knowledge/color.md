# Color correction, grading and scopes

## Measurement and visual intent work together
Color correction aims to resolve unwanted exposure or color differences and make shots work together. Grading can establish an intentional visual style. In practice those activities overlap, but separating technical repair from stylistic decisions makes experiments easier to evaluate. A strong look does not prove that a mismatched input interpretation has been corrected.

Scopes provide measurements that complement the image display. A waveform helps examine signal levels across the picture; a histogram summarizes a distribution rather than showing where each part of the scene lies. An RGB parade separates channel information, while a vectorscope helps inspect chromatic relationships and saturation. Their usefulness depends on interpreting the correct signal and scale. A scope is not a creative instruction to make every shot identical.

## Original shot-matching exercise
Imagine a short interview alternating between two cameras. One angle looks cooler and slightly darker. Begin by checking that the clips are interpreted appropriately. Select comparable moments, compare the subject and background, and change one control at a time. Use scopes to see whether a change affects levels or channels as expected, then judge the cut between shots. Matching only a wall's numerical values may not preserve the most important part of the scene.

The scope might show a concentration of bright values because a white object is in the shot, not because all exposure is wrong. Likewise, different red, green and blue readings do not establish that every pixel should be neutral. The scene's contents matter when deciding what a measurement means.

1. Keep a reversible baseline before applying a creative look.
2. Confirm the intended source and output color interpretations.
3. Evaluate exposure and neutral references where they are actually appropriate.
4. Compare adjacent shots rather than correcting each in isolation.
5. Inspect the final output on the intended playback path.

## Misconceptions and boundaries
A vectorscope is not a license to force all skin or lighting into a single numerical position. A LUT is not a universal repair for any log image, and scopes do not remove the effect of a poorly configured display. This guide intentionally avoids HDR mastering limits, camera-specific transforms and exact Resolve node recipes. Those claims require delivery specifications and additional verified documentation. It explains how to reason with measurements without turning a diagram into an aesthetic rule.
